Common Prey Of Owls

MICE

Mice

Mice are a staple prey item for many owl species, especially barn owls, which are so effective at controlling rodent populations that farmers often install nest boxes to attract them. A single barn owl family can consume over a thousand mice in a single breeding season.

VOLES

Voles

Voles are small, ground-dwelling rodents that make up a huge portion of the diet for grassland and farmland owls, including short-eared and long-eared owls. Vole populations rise and fall in multi-year cycles that owl numbers often track closely.

INSECTS

Insects

Smaller owl species rely heavily on insects rather than mammals, with beetles, moths, and grasshoppers making up a large share of their diet. The tiny elf owl hunts almost entirely by snatching insects off desert vegetation at night.

SMALL BIRDS

Small birds

Larger owls will prey on other birds, including songbirds, waterfowl, and even smaller raptors. The great horned owl is notably aggressive, occasionally taking prey as large as ospreys or peregrine falcons.

The Life of an Owl

Hunting

Owls are among the most efficient nocturnal predators on Earth, having evolved a suite of adaptations that make them nearly undetectable to prey until the moment of the strike. Their wing feathers have soft, comb-like serrated edges along the leading edge and a velvety texture across the surface, which breaks up turbulent airflow and muffles the sound of flight almost entirely — a feature no other bird of prey possesses to the same degree. This allows an owl to glide silently just feet above the ground while scanning for movement below. Once prey is located, owls strike with remarkable speed and precision, using powerful talons capable of exerting several hundred pounds of pressure per square inch — more than enough to instantly kill most small mammals. Many species also practice "sit-and-wait" hunting, perching motionless for long stretches before launching a sudden, silent attack, while others, like the short-eared owl, actively patrol open fields at low altitude in search of movement in the grass.

Vision

An owl's eyes are so large relative to its skull that if a human had eyes proportioned the same way, they would be roughly the size of grapefruits. This enormous eye size allows far more light to enter, giving owls extraordinary night vision — some species can see in light levels roughly 100 times dimmer than what a human needs to see clearly. Their retinas are also packed with rod cells, which are highly sensitive to low light, though this comes at the cost of poor color vision compared to many other birds. However, this size comes with a tradeoff: owl eyes are held rigidly in place by bony structures called sclerotic rings, meaning they cannot move their eyes within their sockets like humans can. To compensate, owls have evolved extraordinarily flexible necks, with roughly double the number of vertebrae found in the human neck. This allows them to rotate their heads up to 270 degrees in either direction, giving them an almost complete field of view without ever needing to move their eyes at all.

Hearing

Owl hearing is arguably even more advanced than their vision, and for many species, it is their primary hunting sense. Owls have large, often asymmetrical ear openings — in species like the barn owl, one ear sits higher on the skull than the other. This asymmetry means sound reaches each ear at a very slightly different time and volume, giving the owl the ability to calculate the precise horizontal and vertical location of a sound with pinpoint accuracy, even a small rodent moving beneath a thick layer of snow or dense grass, entirely out of sight. Many owls also have a facial disc — the flat, circular arrangement of feathers around their face — which functions much like a satellite dish, funneling sound waves toward their ear openings and amplifying faint noises. In laboratory studies, barn owls have been shown to catch prey in complete darkness using hearing alone, striking with an accuracy comparable to hunting in full daylight. This combination of directional accuracy and amplification makes owl hearing one of the most sophisticated auditory systems in the animal kingdom.

Habitat

Owls are among the most widely distributed birds on the planet, found on every continent except Antarctica, and occupying an extraordinary range of environments as a result. Dense forests are home to species like the spotted owl and tawny owl, which rely on tree cavities and thick canopy cover for nesting and camouflage. Open grasslands and prairies support ground-nesting species such as the short-eared owl and burrowing owl, which have adapted to hunt over exposed terrain rather than swooping down from tree branches. Even the harshest environments have their specialists: the snowy owl thrives in the treeless Arctic tundra, hunting lemmings across open snowfields, while several small owl species have adapted to arid deserts, nesting in cactus cavities or rock crevices to escape the heat of the day. Owls have also proven remarkably adaptable to environments shaped by humans. Species like the barn owl and eastern screech owl are frequently found nesting in barns, church steeples, abandoned buildings, and even suburban parks, taking advantage of artificial structures that mimic the tree hollows and cliff ledges they would naturally use. This adaptability, combined with the fact that different species have evolved to fill nearly every available ecological niche, is a major reason owls have become one of the most successful and widespread groups of birds of prey in the world.
